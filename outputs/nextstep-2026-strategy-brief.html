Phase 1 — Hackathon intelligence brief

1a. Theme decoding

Surface reading: “Build something environmental.” Named sub-domains: climate resilience, renewable energy, conservation, sustainable agriculture, waste reduction.

What it is actually asking for. Two lines do the real work:

“identify a pressing environmental problem affecting your community or the world”
“We encourage you to collaborate with local organizations, environmental groups, researchers, or community members to ensure your solution addresses real-world needs.”
Combined with Adherence — “Does it implement this theme fully or just partially?” — a generic global-abstraction project scores as “partial.” A project anchored to a specific, named place and a specific, named group of people scores as “full.” This is the cheapest scoring point in the rubric and most teams will miss it.

Phase 4 — Top 3 deep dives

① · Score 27

CoolBlock — “Where should the next 40 trees go?”

The problem

A neighborhood association or a two-person city sustainability office has a real but small budget — say $50,000 — for heat mitigation, and no defensible way to spend it. They can see on a public map that their blocks are hot; what they cannot answer is which specific parcels, if planted or shaded, deliver the most cooling to the most heat-vulnerable people per dollar.

Measurable cost: low-income blocks in some Northeast areas run 4.0 °C hotter with 30% less tree cover; an estimated 62 million fewer trees in low-income blocks; 92% of US cities have more residents of color and higher poverty in their hottest neighborhoods.

Evidence of real pain

  1. PLOS ONE — tree cover and temperature disparity across 5,723 communities (doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0249715). The 4.0 °C / 30%-less-canopy figures.
  2. Nature Communications — trees halve UHI globally but cooling benefits accrue to higher incomes and suburbs (s41467-026-71825-x; Dryad doi:10.5061/dryad.905qfttz0).
  3. npj Urban Sustainability — increasing canopy lowers urban air temperature up to 1.5 °C (s42949-025-00277-x). Establishes intervention→degrees for the optimizer.
  4. Scientific Reports — street trees mitigate urban heat exposure (s41598-024-51921-y).
  5. American Forests Tree Equity Score — Urban Heat Equity data short; 62M-tree gap; 92%-of-cities finding.

Five sources, all findable, all peer-reviewed or institutional. Strongest evidence base in the pool.

Existing solutions and why they fail

  • Tree Equity Score: Excellent scoring/advocacy instrument. Does not ingest a budget, find plantable space, rank parcels, or produce a plan. The disparity has persisted while the map was public — mapping was never the bottleneck.
  • Municipal urban forest master plans: Rigorous PDFs, city-wide, updated every 10 years — unusable by the group holding $50,000.
  • i-Tree (USDA): Scientific gold standard for quantifying benefits of trees you already have. Calculator, not a siting optimizer. UX assumes a trained forester.

Proposed solution direction

A block-scale heat-mitigation siting optimizer. Drop a pin, enter a budget. The system:

  1. Pulls Landsat/Sentinel land-surface temperature → heat surface.
  2. Pulls OSM/Overpass footprints + Census/ACS demographics + Tree Equity Score canopy.
  3. Segments aerial imagery for actually plantable space — the step that separates this from every existing map.
  4. Runs constrained optimization: maximize (cooling × vulnerable population) subject to budget.
  5. Renders ranked sites + before/after modeled heat surface + Claude-generated council-ready memo.
Core insight: Everyone has built the map. Nobody has built the plan. The gap is not “which blocks are hot” — it is “given $50,000 and this geography, here are the 40 parcels, ranked, with modeled degrees each one buys.”

3-minute demo: pin drop → heat surface → budget slider → optimizer runs → 40 ranked pins → before/after toggle → memo generates. Map on screen inside 20 seconds.

Tech: Claude for structured-data-to-narrative (council memo) + tool use over parcels — not chat. Wolfram for unit-aware geospatial computation and optimization.

Submission fit

  • C1 strongest in pool — heat map is instantly legible
  • C2 pass — Landsat, OSM, Census, Tree Equity — all free
  • C3 pass — map-first UI
  • C4 pass — segmentation + fusion + optimization = four components
  • C5 automatic — name a real neighborhood
  • C6 watch — requires disciplined scoping
  • C7 pass · C8 pass

Judging alignment

  • Originality: decision tool in a field of measurement tools.
  • Adherence (fully): concrete intervention + named community.
  • Completion: one neighborhood end-to-end beats five cities half-working.
  • Technology: five distinct components; before/after heat surface = “wow.”

Judge resonance

  • Chilakala (Data & AI): multi-source fusion is his job. Frame: “the hard part was the join, not the model.”
  • Desai (Microsoft AI): evaluate segmentation and optimizer; show failure modes.
  • Mahajan (Visa): “$50,000 budget, 40 ranked parcels, degrees per dollar.” Lead the video with the cost figure.

Framing: “The data has been public for ten years and the disparity hasn’t moved. That’s not a data problem — it’s an allocation problem. So I built the allocator.”

Risks. Degrees-per-canopy relationships may not transfer at block scale — validate heat surface against known hot spots in 3 days; if wrong, downgrade to “prioritization score.” Cache Tree Equity data day 1. Scope is the real failure mode: half-built pipeline on Sep 13. Avoid Google Earth Engine. Precompute all rasters for the demo neighborhood. CRS/projection is where LLM code fails silently — budget hand-debug time.

Competitive positioning: requires geospatial data engineering outside the LLM-app comfort zone. Non-obvious angle: measurement → allocation. Barrier: plantable-space segmentation + optimizer formulation.

② · Score 23

SolarGhost — “Your panels have been losing money for four months.”

The problem

A homeowner with rooftop solar checks their inverter app, sees a production number, and has no idea whether it is a good number. A tree grows into the peak-production window, or soiling builds up, and they silently lose generation.

Measurable cost: unmonitored systems lose an estimated 10–20% of annual generation; a single bird dropping on one cell of a string-inverter system can cost 5–25% of that string’s production.

Evidence of real pain

  1. Team Solar Works — “Silent Losses” (10–20% annual loss figure).
  2. US Power Solar — “black box” homeowner experience; bird-dropping / string-loss mechanism.
  3. SAMS MD — shading drift over time as trees grow.
  4. US Patent 10,998,853 — IoT-enabled solar PV health monitoring.
  5. US Patent 12,017,256 — PV soil monitoring with automated clean referencing.

Honest note: three of five sources are vendor blogs with commercial interest. Weaker than P1’s peer-reviewed base — why Pain scored 4 not 5.

Existing solutions and why they fail

  • Enphase / SolarEdge / Tesla apps: show actual production with no expected baseline. 40 kWh means nothing without “you should have made 52.” Attribution absent.
  • Professional O&M platforms (Also Energy, Raptor Maps): solve this for utility/commercial fleets. Residential homeowner cannot buy or operate one.
  • Manual PVWatts: free, NREL-backed, right physics — requires tilt, azimuth, derate factors, monthly cross-reference. Effectively nobody does this.

Proposed solution direction

An expected-vs-actual attribution engine. Build a physics-based expected-generation curve from NASA POWER / PVWatts, compare against actual production, classify the residual: soiling (gradual, recovers after rain), shading (time-of-day-specific, seasonally drifting), inverter fault (step change), or weather (already accounted for).

Core insight: the signal isn’t the production number — it’s the shape of the gap over time. Soiling, shading, and hardware faults leave distinct temporal fingerprints.

3-minute demo: load production history → expected curve overlays → residual highlighted → classifier labels three events → “shading, east string, onset mid-June, 340 kWh / $61 lost to date.”

Tech: Claude for diagnostic explanation + recommended action. Physics from PVWatts / NASA POWER. Use pvlib, not hand-rolled solar math.

Submission fit

Strong on C1–C4, C6–C8. Weakest on C5 — “homeowners with solar” is a population, not a place. Mitigate by anchoring to a named city’s solar stock and real weather data.

Judge resonance

Chilakala & Desai: strongest pure-ML resonance of the three. Residual decomposition + fault classification is a canonical industrial ML pattern. Tesla judge (2025): direct solar/energy adjacency. Parth Jain (Netflix): time-series anomaly detection.

Framing: “Every inverter app shows you production. None of them show you the counterfactual. I built the counterfactual, then classified the gap.”

Risks. Validate that the three failure modes are separable in the residual on a real public dataset in 3 days. Confirm NREL / PVOutput access day 1. Solar geometry and timezone handling are where LLM code is confidently wrong. Demo against a curated window with verified ground-truth events.
③ · Score 23

TrueAir — “Your neighborhood’s air data is wrong in a way nobody told you.”

The problem

Community groups in environmental-justice neighborhoods rely on low-cost PurpleAir sensors — often the only monitoring their area has. Raw readings carry substantial humidity-driven bias, and the EPA’s national correction is materially less accurate in humid regions. Friction moment: bringing sensor data to a regulator and having its accuracy challenged.

Measurable cost: 30,000+ networked PurpleAir sensors; region-appropriate correction reduces error metrics by 16–23% vs. EPA’s national model in humid conditions.

Evidence of real pain

  1. AMT vol. 17, p. 6735 (2024) — PurpleAir calibration under high RH; 16–23% error reduction over Barkjohn model.
  2. AMT vol. 16, p. 1311 (2023) — evaluation of EPA correction in smoke, dust, wintertime pollution.
  3. PMC11859157 — AI/ML models for calibrating PurpleAir; ML calibration is live research.
  4. PMC10329730 — frames low-cost sensors as an equity problem.
  5. Data in Brief — Beirut PurpleAir calibration dataset; usable public reference.

Five peer-reviewed sources. Gap is unusually crisply documented — EPA published the correction; literature quantifies exactly where it fails.

Existing solutions and why they fail

  • EPA AirNow + Barkjohn correction: real achievement, but developed with few southeastern US humid sites. Single national equation cannot be locally optimal everywhere.
  • PurpleAir’s own map: offers conversion dropdowns with no guidance on which is right for local conditions.
  • Regulatory reference monitors: ground truth — but sparse, which is why community networks exist.

Proposed solution direction

A locally-calibrated community air quality service. Pair nearby PurpleAir sensors with the closest regulatory reference monitor, train a local correction model (RH + temperature predictors), quantify improvement over EPA national equation on held-out data, republish corrected readings with honest uncertainty bounds + a methodology note a community group can hand to a regulator.

Core insight: the correction should be learned locally, not applied nationally. Output is not just better numbers — it is defensible numbers.

3-minute demo: map of sensors → toggle raw / EPA-corrected / locally-corrected against reference ground truth → error metric drops → methodology note generates.

Submission fit

Good on C2–C4, C6–C8. C1 is the weak point — “our regression has lower RMSE” is hardest to make thrilling in 45 seconds. Mitigate by leading with map recoloring as correction applies. C5: choose a named EJ neighborhood.

Judge resonance

Chilakala & Desai: held-out validation against a real baseline with quantified improvement — empirically verifiable on screen. Rare in hackathons. Mahajan: equity framing + “defensible in front of a regulator.”

Framing: “The EPA published one equation for the whole country. The literature says it’s 16–23% worse where it’s humid. So I learned the local one, and here’s the held-out validation.”

Risks. Day-1 dependency: sufficient co-located PurpleAir + regulatory pairs with overlapping history. If pairs don’t exist, project has no ground truth. You may reproduce the published result rather than improve — frame as replication-plus-localization if so. Sensor–monitor spatial/temporal join is where LLM code silently misaligns. Request PurpleAir API key day 1; demo entirely offline from a local snapshot.

Phase 5 — Final recommendation

Build CoolBlock (P1 — neighborhood heat-mitigation siting optimizer)

It wins over SolarGhost and TrueAir on the two things that actually decide this event. First, the demo: a map with a modeled before/after heat surface is legible in fifteen seconds to a volunteer judge on their ninth video — whereas SolarGhost’s residual chart and TrueAir’s error metric both require comprehension before they can impress, and comprehension is the scarcest resource in the room. Second, it is the only one of the three that satisfies Adherence’s “fully vs. partially” test automatically, because you will name a real neighborhood and a real budget.

It is built for this panel: Chilakala and Desai will recognize genuine multi-source raster-to-vector-to-demographic fusion as real engineering; Mahajan hears “$50,000, 40 ranked parcels, degrees per dollar” as a resource-allocation problem in her language. It clears Technology’s “many different components” test with five distinct ones. Realistically it competes for 1st place and the YC interview. Best available sponsor-signal via deep Claude + almost-universally-ignored Wolfram.

Biggest risk is scope — most ambitious of the three; failure mode is a half-built pipeline on Sep 13. Manage by fixing scope to one named neighborhood, end to end, on day one and refusing to generalize.

In the first 72 hours, you must:

  1. 1. Pick the neighborhood and lock it. Today. One city, one district, named. Never expand it.
  2. 2. Cache every data source locally — Landsat/Sentinel LST, OSM footprints, Census/ACS, Tree Equity Score — for that neighborhood only. After day 3 the demo should never touch a live API.
  3. 3. Validate the heat surface against reality. Does it reproduce known hot spots? If not, downgrade from “predicted cooling” to “prioritization score” and say so plainly.
  4. 4. Resolve CRS/projection alignment by hand. Where LLM geospatial code fails silently. Do it before any UI.
  5. 5. Start the Devpost writeup on day 3, not day 10. Only channel that scores Learning. Both 2025 winners wrote ~2,500 structured words. Most underrated deliverable in this event.
Monitor, don’t act yet: Judges tab (panel unpublished — if it shifts toward environmental experts, re-weight toward scientific defensibility) and Updates / Discord for a possible Kinetik category announcement.

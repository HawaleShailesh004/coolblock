Phase 1 — Hackathon intelligence brief

1a. Theme decoding

“Earth Forward” is an environmental-tech theme. The description lists climate resilience, renewable energy, conservation, sustainable agriculture, and waste reduction. The key phrase is identify a pressing environmental problem affecting your community or the world. That is intentionally broad — any environmental angle qualifies. The encouragement to collaborate with local organizations, environmental groups, researchers, or community members signals judges reward grounded, community-connected solutions over abstract global platforms.

What the theme implicitly favors: tangible, demonstrable environmental impact on a specific community or user group. Not policy papers, not awareness campaigns — working tools that address a specific pain point.

Disqualifiers / constraints: Students only, ages 13+. Companies and professional orgs excluded. Old projects allowed only if you clearly delineate pre-hackathon vs. during-hackathon work.

1b. Participant density read

502 participants is moderate for a Devpost hackathon. In past years: 2025 had 885, 2024 had a similar scale. Actual submissions will be far fewer — typically 15–25% of registrants submit. Expect ~75–125 submissions.

At this count, a polished, technically impressive project with a clear problem statement stands out. The average team is high-school students, many first-time hackers (the event is tagged Beginner Friendly). The quality floor is low — many submissions will be concept pitches, half-working prototypes, or generic “awareness” apps. A fully functional demo with real data wins on completion alone.

Phase 2 — Solution space mapping

2a. Track selection

There is only one track: Earth Forward (environmental). No track selection needed.

2b. Default solution (40–60% of teams)

“An eco-awareness web app that calculates your personal carbon footprint from a questionnaire, displays it on a dashboard with charts, and suggests generic tips to reduce it. May include a chatbot powered by an LLM that answers sustainability questions. Basic React / Next.js frontend, a few API calls, deployed on Vercel.”

Variants: recycling classifier (upload image → “plastic” or “glass”), tree-planting tracker, sustainability-tips chatbot, environmental news aggregator.

This is the crowded center. Do not build this.

2c. Existing solution landscape

  • Carbon calculators: EPA, CoolClimate, Wren, Joro, Klima — saturated.
  • Recycling classifiers: TrashBot and countless hackathon projects. Overdone.
  • Air quality: PurpleAir, AirNow, IQAir — extensive networks already exist.
  • Water quality: Limited consumer tools. EPA databases cover public systems, not private wells. Genuine gap.
  • Food waste: Too Good To Go, Flashfood, Winnow, Leanpath. Household tools are weak.
  • Biodiversity: iNaturalist, eBird, Seek — dominant, with documented bias and offline limits.
  • Urban heat: Academic tools exist; no consumer-facing community tools. Gap.

Phase 4 — Top 3 problem deep dives

#1 · Score 25

WellSafe — Private well water risk intelligence

The problem

23 million US households rely on private wells. The EPA has no authority to regulate or monitor them. Testing costs $100–300+ per panel, requires mailing samples, and returns jargon most homeowners can’t interpret. 40% of Iowa well owners never test. PFAS — linked to cancer, thyroid disease, and immune suppression — has been found in 20–95% of tested private wells depending on region. Low-income and minority communities test least.

Evidence of real pain

  1. PFAS in Rural US Well Water (2025, ES&T): “The vast majority of private well users lack information to guide decision-making… public health departments are typically understaffed.” doi:10.1021/acs.est.5c02521
  2. “Bad water just means bad health” — NC barriers study (2025): Financial barriers, agricultural hazards, discrimination, knowledge gaps, mistrust. doi:10.1088/2752-5309/ae4f17
  3. Iowa Private Well Testing RCT (2024, ES&T): “Around 40% of households do not regularly test, treat, or avoid their drinking water.” doi:10.1021/acs.est.4c02835
  4. Private Well Water Safety (2025, JABFM): Low-income and less-educated households test and treat less, even when free testing exists. JABFM 38/6
  5. Tapwater contaminant mixtures (2025, RSC): High analytical costs and conflation of safety with taste undermine monitoring. doi:10.1039/D5EW00490J

Existing solutions and why they fail

  • EPA PFAS Analytic Tools: National source database, not a user tool. Doesn’t estimate private-well contamination likelihood.
  • State well-testing programs: Understaffed, limited contaminants, affluent participation bias.
  • MyWell / private well apps: Record-keeping, not risk prediction.
  • Home test kits (Tap Score): Still $100–200+, mail-in, technical results. Don’t predict risk before you test.
The gap: No tool tells a well owner, from location, well depth, land use, and known sources, what to test first — before they spend money.

Proposed solution direction

WellSafe — AI-powered well water risk assessment. Enter an address and answer 5–6 questions (depth, age, nearby land use). Cross-references EPA PFAS / Superfund data, USGS groundwater quality, agricultural land use, aquifer vulnerability maps, and known incidents. Claude writes a personalized plain-language risk report with a cheap first test.

Winning 3-minute demo: map → address → risk layers → report → prioritized tests → upload a lab PDF and have Claude interpret it.

Core insight: risk predictor, not test-result recorder. Uses public data that has never been assembled for consumers.

Special prize stack

No special-category prizes in 2026. Deep Claude use (synthesis, report, lab interpretation) may informally resonate because Claude is a sponsor.

Submission fit

  • C1 pass — Map UI with risk layers and reports
  • C2 pass — Standard web app on Vercel / Railway
  • C3 pass — “23M households don’t know what’s in their water → enter address → risk → action”
  • C4 pass — Next.js + API routes + Claude SDK
  • C5 pass — Water and community health
  • C6 pass — Built from scratch during the hackathon

Judging criterion alignment

  • Originality: High. Carbon calculators are everywhere; well-water risk predictors are not.
  • Adherence to track: Full. Water safety + community engagement.
  • Completion: Web app + public APIs + Claude. Achievable in 3 weeks.
  • Learning: Multi-source fusion, geospatial viz, AI reports.
  • Design: Layered risk maps are visually compelling.
  • Technology: Impressive without being fragile.

Judge resonance

  • Aditya: Accessibility and social impact; underserved users; “real product” feel.
  • Krishnan: Multi-data-source architecture.
  • Panel: “23 million households” lands. Past winners targeted underserved populations.
Honest risk flags. USGS / EPA / Superfund data may need bulk download — verify coverage in 48 hours. Geospatial accuracy may force county-level, not parcel-level. Include “not a substitute for professional testing.”
Integration risk. EPA, USGS, Claude, and geocoding APIs are low risk. Most likely demo failure: address with no coverage. Mitigation: pre-load 3–4 demo regions and a graceful insufficient-data state.

Competitive positioning: Requires environmental-data literacy most high-school teams lack. Default projects are carbon calculators. Moat is data-fusion logic plus domain knowledge.

#2 · Score 23

HeatMap — Neighborhood heat vulnerability intelligence

The problem

Urban heat islands kill more Americans annually than any other weather event. Low-income neighborhoods — less tree cover, more asphalt, older housing without AC — run 5–15°F hotter than affluent areas in the same city. Block-level satellite thermal data exists (ECOSTRESS, Landsat), but a review of 500+ papers found only 10.9% engaged the communities analyzed. Data exists; translation to action does not.

Evidence of real pain

  1. “Valley of death” in urban heat research (2025): 10.9% of 500+ papers engaged communities. doi:10.1177/19394071251413391
  2. UHI policy in 5 cities (2025, Royal Society): Science-to-practice translation remains limited; planners need simple tools. doi:10.1098/rsta.2024.0581
  3. Bridging the application gap (2026): Impasse is not a deficit of tools. doi:10.1177/2754124x261447620
  4. Climate services reframing: Providers struggle to serve users unlike themselves. ScienceDirect

Existing solutions and why they fail

  • NASA ECOSTRESS Explorer: Raw imagery, GIS expertise, no guidance.
  • Heat.gov (NIHHIS): Coarse, not neighborhood-specific.
  • Academic heat-equity maps: In journals, not in residents’ hands.
The gap: No consumer tool says “your block is X degrees hotter, here’s why, here are the interventions that would help most.”

Proposed solution direction

HeatMap — enter an address, get a block heat-risk score from Landsat / ECOSTRESS, tree canopy, impervious surface, census vulnerability, and AC estimates. Claude writes a neighborhood heat profile with ranked interventions.

Demo: address → animated heat map → “Your block is 8°F hotter… 15 street trees on Main St would cut peak temp ~3°F.”

All submission constraints satisfied. Heat maps demo extremely well.

Judging / judges

  • Originality high for a student hackathon. Direct climate-resilience and environmental-justice fit.
  • Same student panel; environmental-justice angle is strong; visuals win non-specialists.
Risks. ECOSTRESS / Landsat needs rasterio / GDAL — hardest 20%. Pre-process a few demo cities. Surface temperature ≠ air temperature — say so. NASA Earthdata is medium risk. Most likely demo failure: tile timeout. Mitigation: pre-render static tiles.

Competitive positioning: Satellite processing is a real barrier at this participant level. A GIS-experienced team could copy it; that is unlikely here.

#3 · Score 22

FreshLens — AI expiry intelligence for zero food waste

The problem

US households waste ~30–40% of food purchased, often because “best by,” “sell by,” and “use by” get treated as hard expiry. Only “use by” relates to safety. USDA estimate: $444 per person per year thrown away while still safe. Existing apps need tedious manual entry and ignore storage conditions.

Evidence of real pain

  1. Food waste tracking accuracy (2025): Technology alone doesn’t fix reporting if staff lack time, clarity, or incentive. study
  2. IoT food waste review (MDPI 2023): Data quality, standardization, security, connectivity barriers. doi:10.3390/su15043482
  3. Technica 2025 postmortem (“freshly yours”): Receipt scanning and food-item verification were painful. github.com/candace-sun/technica-2025
  4. Re-Plate: Label formats vary; needed vision + OCR. github.com/Arjun-Mishra-312/re-plate

Existing solutions and why they fail

  • Too Good To Go / Flashfood: Business surplus, not household waste.
  • Fridgely / NoWaste: Manual entry kills adoption; generic shelf life.
  • Receipt scanners: Unreliable parsing; weak food-item mapping.
The gap: No tool lets you point a camera at the fridge and get real (not label) expiry plus “use first” recipes.

Proposed solution direction

FreshLens — camera on fridge / pantry. Claude Vision IDs items, estimates remaining freshness from type + storage, then a prioritized use-first list with recipes.

Demo: snap fridge → “peppers ~2 days; yogurt fine (best-by is quality, not safety); tonight: stuffed peppers.”

Judging / judges

  • Originality medium-high. Risk: “another food waste app” if positioning slips. Lead with “your food is still safe.”
  • Photo Cook won 2nd in 2022 with picture-of-ingredients → recipe. This panel has rewarded that UX.
Risks. Vision may confuse similar foods — test in 48 hours. Generic recipes flatten the demo. Camera permissions can fail live. Mitigation: pre-captured images as fallback.

Competitive positioning: Photo Cook clones will cluster. Moat is expiry science, not “what can I cook.”

Phase 5 — Final recommendation

Bet on WellSafe — private well water risk intelligence

It has the highest total score (25), driven by five 2024–2025 peer-reviewed studies and the widest solution gap: no consumer tool does this. The architecture is the most demo-stable of the three — web app, public APIs, Claude text synthesis — no camera, no satellite rasters, no ambiguous computer vision. It is the most original in this field: judges will have seen food-waste apps and dashboards; they will not have seen a well-water risk predictor. The 3-minute story is brutal: “23 million Americans don’t know what’s in their water. EPA can’t help them. Enter your address. Now you know.” Claude use is deep and natural (multi-source synthesis, not a chatbot wrapper), which fits a Claude-sponsored event.

Biggest risk: data coverage. If the demo address falls in a gap, the experience breaks. Pre-load 3–4 counties with known contamination and build an insufficient-data fallback.

First 72 hours: (1) verify EPA PFAS source API and USGS groundwater coverage; (2) build geocoding → fusion for one demo county; (3) get Claude generating a sample report from real data. If data access works, this project wins.
